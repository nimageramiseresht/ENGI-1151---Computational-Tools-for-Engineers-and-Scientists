Part A
Fitting distributions to data

Part A: the theory and the tools of distribution fitting and goodness of fit.

1From a histogram to a distribution

empirical datahistogramtheoretical distribution

A simulation (or a set of measurements) gives us thousands of numbers. The histogram shows their shape, but it is discrete: bars, not a formula.

A fitted distribution replaces the bars with a smooth curve and a few parameters. It lets us:

  • calculate probabilities and percentiles anywhere, even beyond the observed data;
  • describe thousands of results with 2 or 3 numbers;
  • reuse the result as an input to another simulation.
Recap: after a Monte Carlo simulation (typically 1 000 to 100 000 runs) we interpret the results with the mean or median, the standard deviation, histograms, percentiles and the probability of exceeding a threshold. Histograms, in their ordinary or cumulative form, are discrete: bars over bins. That makes some questions awkward. The example is 200 records of pump repair times. The longest observed repair was 35 hours. What is the chance that a repair takes more than 40 hours? The histogram has no bars there, so it says zero, which is surely wrong. Distribution fitting finds a known, smooth probability distribution (normal, gamma, beta, …) that matches the data. Then we can answer such questions from a formula, summarise all the results with two or three parameters, and feed the fitted distribution into another simulation as an input. Two main uses in engineering: fitting distributions to real data to define the inputs of a simulation, and fitting distributions to the outputs of a simulation to analyse them.

1What is distribution fitting?

distribution familyparameters θfitting

Each family has its own shape, its pdf \(f(x \mid \theta)\); its parameters \(\theta\) move and stretch it. Fitting chooses the family and the parameters that match the data.

The workflow for this whole lecture, in five steps. 1. Data: measured values, or the outputs of a simulation. 2. Candidates: which families of distributions could describe the data? Is it symmetric or skewed, bounded or unbounded? Section 2 gives a guide. 3. Fit: for each candidate, find the parameter values that match the data best. The standard method is maximum likelihood (next slides). 4. Test: how good is each fit? The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (section 3). Pick the best candidate. 5. Use the fitted distribution: percentiles, probabilities of exceeding limits, or as an input to the next simulation. The graph shows six families on the same axis, all with similar typical values but very different shapes: the normal is symmetric and unbounded; lognormal, gamma and Weibull are positive and right-skewed, each in its own way; the exponential is highest at zero; the uniform is flat between two limits. Each family is a formula, the probability density function f(x | θ), read "f of x given theta", where θ stands for all its parameters: (μ, σ) for the normal, (a, scale) for the gamma. The parameters shift, stretch and bend the curve. Fitting means choosing a family and then the parameter values under which our data are most likely.

1.1Likelihood: how well do the parameters explain the data?

likelihoodprobability density f(x | θ)maximisation

The idea: find the parameters whose curve best suits the histogram of our results. The way: measure how likely our data are under given parameters, the likelihood \(L(\theta)\), and choose the parameters that maximise it.

\(L(\theta) = \displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i \mid \theta)\)

Multiply the heights of the curve at every data point. Higher L: these parameters explain the data better.

Why a likelihood? The idea of distribution fitting is to find the parameters that make the fitted curve suit the histogram of our results as well as possible. We need a single number that measures how well a given set of parameters explains the data; then fitting becomes "make that number as large as possible". That number is the likelihood. Suppose we have n observations x1 … xn and we assume they come independently from a distribution with probability density f(x | θ), where θ are the unknown parameters. The likelihood is the joint probability density of observing all our data for given parameters: the product of the densities at every data point, L(θ) = Π f(xi | θ). Widget: eight data points, their histogram (light bars) and a normal curve. Each red line is the height of the curve at a data point. The likelihood multiplies all eight heights. Move μ: when the curve sits over the data, the heights are larger and L grows. Move σ: too narrow and the outer points get almost zero height; too wide and every height is small. The best compromise is in between. Click "maximum likelihood": the curve jumps to the parameters with the largest L. For the normal distribution these are the sample mean and the standard deviation (dividing by n), as we derive on the next slide. Note how small L is (about 6 × 10 to the minus 7 at its maximum): products of many small numbers become tiny. With thousands of data points they underflow to zero on a computer. That is why we use the logarithm.

1.2Log-likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation

log-likelihoodMLEarg max

The product is numerically unstable, so take the natural log. Log turns a product into a sum and does not move the maximum:

\(\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln f(x_i \mid \theta)\)

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the parameter vector that maximises it:

\(\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta}\ \ell(\theta)\)

At the maximum, every partial derivative is zero:

\(\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_j} = 0, \quad j = 1, \dots, k\)

Example: the normal distribution

\(\ell(\mu, \sigma) = -n \ln\!\big(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\big) - \dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2\)

\(\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu) = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \hat{\mu} = \bar{x}\)

\(\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma} = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2\)

The MLEs of the normal are the sample mean and the standard deviation (dividing by n): exactly what the widget found.

Because the likelihood is a product of many small numbers, it is numerically unstable. Take the natural logarithm: the log of a product is the sum of the logs, ℓ(θ) = Σ ln f(xi | θ). The logarithm is monotonically increasing, so the parameters that maximise ℓ also maximise L: maximising either gives the same answer, the maximum likelihood estimate θ-hat. To find the maximum, differentiate with respect to each parameter and set the derivatives to zero: a system of k equations for k parameters. Normal example (from the lecture handout): substitute the normal pdf, take logs, differentiate. The derivative with respect to μ is zero when Σ(xi − μ) = 0, so μ-hat is the sample mean. The derivative with respect to σ gives σ-hat squared = (1/n) Σ (xi − x̄)². Note 1/n, not 1/(n − 1): this is what np.std gives by default. For the normal distribution the equations have a closed-form solution. For most other distributions (gamma, beta, Weibull) they do not, which is where computation comes in.

1.3Computational fitting: climbing the likelihood hill

numerical optimisationclosed formmethod of moments

For most families (gamma, beta, Weibull, …) the equations \(\partial \ell / \partial \theta_j = 0\) have no closed-form solution. The computer solves them numerically:

  1. start from a reasonable guess of the parameters;
  2. calculate \(\ell(\theta)\) for the data;
  3. move the parameters a little in the direction that increases \(\ell\);
  4. repeat until \(\ell\) stops increasing: the top of the hill is \(\hat{\theta}\).

SciPy’s dist.fit(data) does exactly this: maximum likelihood with a numerical optimiser.

Method of moments, a quick alternative: choose the parameters so that the distribution’s mean and variance equal those of the data. Simple, but usually less accurate than MLE.

For the normal distribution we could solve the equations by hand. For the gamma distribution (and beta, Weibull and most others) the equations have no closed-form solution, so we solve the maximisation numerically. Picture the log-likelihood as a landscape over the parameter space: here the two parameters of a gamma distribution, the shape a (horizontal) and the scale (vertical), for the 200 repair times. Darker colour means higher log-likelihood. A numerical optimiser starts from a guess (black square: a = 1, scale = 9), evaluates ℓ, moves towards higher ℓ and repeats. The red path shows the actual steps of the Nelder–Mead algorithm, which SciPy uses inside dist.fit. After about 45 steps it reaches the top (yellow star): a = 2.68, scale = 3.51. That is the MLE. You never write this loop yourself: stats.gamma.fit(data) does it. But it explains why fitting can occasionally be slow, give warnings, or land on a poor answer from a bad starting point; fixing a parameter you already know (for example floc=0 for data that start at zero) makes the search easier. Method of moments: match the mean and variance of the distribution to those of the data. For the gamma: mean = a × scale and variance = a × scale², which can be solved directly. Quick and useful as a starting guess, but MLE is the standard.

A gallery of distributions

Which candidate families should we try? A tour of the most common distributions in scipy.stats.

2.1Common distributions in scipy.stats

pdfsupportshape, loc, scaleskewness

A tour of the families you will use most, all available in scipy.stats. Choose a distribution and move its sliders. Watch three things: the support (where the curve can be non-zero), the symmetry or skew, and how the tails behave. Normal (norm): symmetric, unbounded in both directions; loc is the mean, scale the standard deviation. Student t: like the normal but heavier tails for small df; use it for data with more extreme values than a normal allows. Uniform: flat between loc and loc + scale. Triangular (triang): bounded, with a most likely value; c sets where the peak is (0 = left end, 1 = right end); popular for expert estimates (minimum, most likely, maximum). Exponential (expon): starts at loc, always right-skewed; times between random events. Lognormal (lognorm): positive, right-skewed; s is the standard deviation of ln x, larger s gives more skew. Gamma: positive, right-skewed for small a, becoming almost symmetric for large a. Weibull (weibull_min): positive; c < 1 decreasing, c ≈ 3.6 almost symmetric; used for wind speeds and failure times. Beta: bounded between 0 and 1 (or loc to loc + scale with SciPy); a > b gives left skew, a < b right skew, a = b symmetric. We used it last week for the turbine efficiencies. SciPy convention: every distribution has loc and scale, plus shape parameters for some families (a, s, c, df …). In .fit results the shape parameters come first, then loc, then scale.

Look at the histogram first (bounds, skew, tails), choose 2 to 4 candidates, fit them all and let the goodness-of-fit test decide.

A practical guide for choosing candidates. Look at your histogram and ask: can the values be negative? Is there a hard upper limit? Which way does the long tail point? Are the values counts? Symmetric and unbounded: normal; t if there are more extreme values than a normal would produce. Bounded: uniform when you only know the range; triangular or beta when there is a most likely value; beta with a > b for data bunched near the upper limit (left-skewed), like last week's efficiencies. Positive and right-skewed, the most common case for engineering quantities that cannot be negative: gamma, lognormal, Weibull. Exponential when most values are near zero, for times between random events. Counts: discrete distributions such as Poisson and binomial (scipy.stats has them too, but they are fitted differently; beyond this module). Physics should also guide the choice: a normal distribution for a quantity that cannot be negative (a repair time, a daily energy) will put probability on impossible negative values. We will see that on the next slides. The guide gives candidates, not answers: always fit several and compare them with a goodness-of-fit test.

Goodness of fit

Fitting always gives an answer. Is it a good answer? The goodness of fit, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

3.1Fitting always gives an answer: is it a good one?

goodness of fitvisual checkstatistical test

stats.norm.fit(data) happily returns μ and σ, even though the data are skewed and cannot be negative. The MLE is the best normal, not necessarily a good description.

Goodness of fit measures how well a fitted distribution matches the data: visually (pdf over the histogram, CDF over the empirical CDF) and with a statistical test: Kolmogorov–Smirnov, chi-square, Anderson–Darling.

Maximum likelihood always returns parameters: it finds the best member of the family you asked for. If the family is wrong, the best member can still be a poor description. Left: the best normal distribution for the repair times. It misses the peak, underestimates the long right tail, and gives a noticeable probability to negative repair times, which are impossible. Right: the best gamma distribution follows the data closely. So after fitting we must check the goodness of fit. First, always look: the fitted pdf over a density histogram, or the fitted CDF over the empirical CDF. Then test it with a statistic that measures the mismatch. Common tests: Kolmogorov–Smirnov (today), chi-square (compares histogram counts) and Anderson–Darling (like KS but more sensitive in the tails).

3.2The empirical CDF

CDFempirical CDF (ECDF)step function

The CDF of a distribution, \(F(x)\), is the probability of a value up to \(x\). It climbs from 0 to 1.

The empirical CDF does the same for the data: the share of data points up to \(x\).

\(F_n(x) = \dfrac{\text{number of } x_i \le x}{n}\)

Sort the data; the ECDF is a staircase that jumps up by \(1/n\) at every data point.

No bins, no choices: the ECDF uses every data point exactly. That is why tests compare CDFs rather than histograms.

To test a fit we compare the data with the distribution. Histograms depend on the choice of bins, so we compare cumulative distributions instead. The CDF F(x) of a distribution is the probability of getting a value less than or equal to x. It always rises from 0 to 1. The empirical CDF Fn(x) of the data is the fraction of data points less than or equal to x. Sort the data: below the smallest point it is 0, and it jumps up by 1/n at every data point, reaching 1 at the largest. Example: eight data points. At x = 6.4, five of the eight points are 6.4 or less, so Fn(6.4) = 5/8 = 0.625. The ECDF needs no bins and uses every data point exactly, which makes it the natural thing to compare with a fitted CDF.

3.3The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test: the largest gap

KS statistic Dsupremumcritical value

\(D_n = \sup_x \big|F_n(x) - F(x)\big|\)

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares the empirical CDF of the data with the CDF of the fitted distribution. Its statistic D is the largest vertical distance between the two curves ("sup" means the largest value over all x). Widget: a sample of n repair times (generated from a skewed distribution). Choose a candidate: the widget fits it to the sample by maximum likelihood, draws its CDF, and marks the largest gap in red. Step 1: compute D. Step 2: find the critical value for the significance level α. For n larger than about 40 and α = 0.05, D_crit = 1.36/√n. Step 3: compare. If D > D_crit, the gap is too large to be chance: reject the distribution. If D ≤ D_crit, we cannot reject it: the data are consistent with the distribution. Start with n = 300. Try the normal: the gap is large, around the peak, and the normal is rejected. Exponential: rejected strongly. Gamma: a small gap, not rejected. Now move n down to 50 or 30 with the normal selected: it usually passes. With more data the critical value shrinks (1/√n), so the test becomes stricter and detects small mismatches; with few data almost any reasonable distribution passes. Click "new sample" to see the randomness of the test itself. The p-value: the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if the distribution were correct. Small p (below α) means reject. Advantages from the handout: KS is non-parametric (it does not assume any particular form of the distribution) and works with small samples.

To choose between candidates: smallest D (equivalently, largest p) wins, after checking the plot.

Two equivalent ways to decide. With the critical value: choose a significance level α, the risk of rejecting a correct distribution, usually 0.05. For n above about 40, the critical value is D_α = c_α/√n with c_α from the table (1.36 for α = 0.05). Larger D than that: reject. Note the wording: if D is small we "cannot reject" the distribution; we never prove it is correct. With the p-value, which SciPy calculates for us: the probability of a gap at least as large as the one observed, if the data really came from the fitted distribution. p below 0.05: reject at the 5 per cent level. SciPy's kstest returns a result object with three main fields: statistic (D), pvalue, and statistic_location, the x value where the largest gap occurs, useful to see where the fit is worst (often the peak or a tail). Recent versions also return statistic_sign: +1 when the empirical CDF is above the fitted CDF at that point (the data have more values there than the fit predicts), −1 when below. Choosing between several candidates: the one with the smallest D (largest p), confirmed by looking at the plots. One caveat for the curious: when the parameters are estimated from the same data, the standard KS p-values are a little too generous (the fit was tuned to the data). It is still a good way to compare candidates.

Fitting and testing in Python

The same workflow with scipy.stats: fit, plot, test.

4.1Fitting with scipy.stats

dist.fitfloc=0frozen distribution

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

np.random.seed(7)
data = np.random.gamma(2.5, 4, 200)   # 200 repair times (h)

normal_params = stats.norm.fit(data)
gamma_params = stats.gamma.fit(data)
print("normal (loc, scale):", np.round(normal_params, 3))
print("gamma (a, loc, scale):", np.round(gamma_params, 3))

g = stats.gamma(*gamma_params)        # a "frozen" distribution
print("P(time > 40 h) = %.5f" % g.sf(40))

stats.<family>.fit(data) returns the MLE as a tuple: shape parameters first, then loc, then scale.

norm(loc = μ, scale = σ)
gamma(a, loc, scale)
lognorm(s, loc, scale)
weibull_min(c, loc, scale)
beta(a, b, loc, scale)

floc=0 fixes loc at 0: right for positive data starting at zero, and an easier search.

stats.gamma(*params) “freezes” the fit: then use .pdf, .cdf, .ppf, .sf, .mean(), .rvs().

scipy.stats has an object for every distribution, all with the same methods. .fit(data) runs maximum likelihood and returns the parameters as a tuple. The order of the tuple: shape parameters first (none for the normal; a for gamma; s for lognormal; c for Weibull; a and b for beta), then loc, then scale. For the normal, loc is μ and scale is σ. floc=0 fixes the location parameter at zero, so only the shape and scale are fitted. Use it for quantities that start at zero (times, energies, loads). Without it, SciPy also searches for a shift, which can make the fit unstable. Freezing: stats.gamma(*gamma_params) creates a distribution object with the fitted parameters built in. The star unpacks the tuple into separate arguments (week 4). Then g.pdf(x), g.cdf(x), g.ppf(q), g.sf(x) (survival function, 1 − cdf), g.mean(), g.rvs(size) all work without repeating the parameters. Run it: the normal fit gives μ = 9.42 h, σ = 5.80 h; the gamma fit a = 2.68, scale = 3.51 h. The gamma answers the question from slide 3: the chance of a repair longer than 40 hours (g.sf(40), the survival function) is about 0.0005, small but not zero.

Percentiles and probabilities from the CDF

Once we have a fitted distribution, its CDF answers the two engineering questions: what value for a given probability, and what probability for a given value.

5.1Reading the CDF both ways

percentileppf (inverse CDF)cdfsf = 1 − cdf

The CDF of the fitted distribution answers two kinds of question. Here: the gamma distribution fitted to the daily energy of last week's wind farm (a = 3.15, scale = 104 MWh), which we will derive ourselves in Part B. Probability to value, a percentile: start on the vertical axis at probability p, go across to the curve, then down to the horizontal axis and read the value. For p = 0.05: 93 MWh. On 5 per cent of days the farm produces less than this; on 95 per cent of days it produces more. That is last week's P95. In SciPy this inverse CDF is called the percent point function, dist.ppf(p). Value to probability: start on the horizontal axis at a value x, go up to the curve, then across to the vertical axis: F(x), the probability of a value up to x. For x = 150 MWh, about 0.15: on 15 per cent of days the farm produces 150 MWh or less. dist.cdf(x). The probability of exceeding x is 1 − F(x), the survival function dist.sf(x). Switch the mode and move the sliders. Notice that the curve is steep in the middle (many days in that range) and flat in the tails. Compared with np.percentile on the raw results, the fitted distribution gives smooth answers, and it can answer questions beyond the range of the simulated values.

5.2Percentiles and probabilities in Python

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

np.random.seed(7)
data = np.random.gamma(2.5, 4, 200)
g = stats.gamma(*stats.gamma.fit(data, floc=0))

print("median:          %.2f h" % g.ppf(0.5))
print("95th percentile: %.2f h" % g.ppf(0.95))
print("from the data:   %.2f h" % np.percentile(data, 95))
print("P(time <= 5 h) = %.3f" % g.cdf(5))
print("P(time > 24 h) = %.4f" % g.sf(24))

Note the percentile convention: ppf takes a probability between 0 and 1; np.percentile takes a percentage between 0 and 100. Run it: median about 8.3 h, 95th percentile about 20.4 h from the fit, the same as the value from the data. The chance of a repair taking more than a full day (24 h) is about 0.02. For a limit within the data, the raw results and the fit give similar answers. For limits in the tail or beyond the data, only the fitted distribution can answer, provided it passed the goodness-of-fit test.

def turbine_power(v, theta, eta, rho=1.225, D=80):
    A = np.pi * D**2 / 4
    return 0.5 * rho * A * eta * (v * np.cos(theta))**3 / 1e6   # MW

ab = np.array([[5, 2], [6, 2], [4, 1.5], [5, 1.5], [7, 2],
               [6, 3], [5, 2.5], [8, 3], [4, 2], [6, 2]])
np.random.seed(1)
N = 10_000                                     # simulated days
v = np.random.normal(8, 1.5, (N, 1))           # m/s
theta = np.random.normal(0, 0.26, (N, 1))      # rad
eta = 0.62 + 0.33 * np.random.beta(ab[:, 0], ab[:, 1], (N, 10))
energy = 24 * turbine_power(v, theta, eta).sum(axis=1)   # MWh/day

print("mean %.0f MWh, empirical P95 %.0f MWh"
      % (energy.mean(), np.percentile(energy, 5)))
plt.hist(energy, bins=60, density=True, color="skyblue",
         edgecolor="black")
plt.xlabel("Energy in one day (MWh)")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.show()

Summary

MLEgoodness of fitKS testppf, cdf

  • Distribution fitting: choose a family, then find the parameters that make the data most likely.
  • Likelihood \(L = \prod f(x_i \mid \theta)\); maximise the log-likelihood \(\ell = \sum \ln f(x_i \mid \theta)\).
  • Closed form for the normal (\(\hat\mu = \bar x\)); a numerical optimiser for most others: dist.fit.
  • Choose candidates from the data: bounds, skew, tails.
  • Fitting always gives an answer: check the goodness of fit, visually and with a test.
  • KS test: \(D\) = largest gap between the ECDF and the fitted CDF; reject if \(D > c_\alpha/\sqrt{n}\) or \(p < \alpha\).
  • stats.kstest returns statistic, pvalue, statistic_location.
  • From the fitted CDF: ppf (probability → value), cdf and sf (value → probability).
Recap. Distribution fitting turns a cloud of simulation results or measurements into a smooth model with a few parameters. Maximum likelihood finds the parameters; for most families SciPy does it numerically. Fitting always returns parameters, so always check the goodness of fit: look at the plots, then use the KS test to reject poor candidates and pick the best one. With the fitted distribution, read percentiles and probabilities from its CDF: ppf, cdf and sf.
