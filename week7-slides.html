What is optimisation?

What optimisation means, the mountain analogy, and why engineers need it.

1Optimisation: find the top of the mountain

objective functiondecision variableoptimumlocal vs global

Optimisation: finding the best solution according to a criterion.

  • Where you stand = the decision variable \(x\): what we can choose.
  • The height = the objective function \(f(x)\): what we want to make as large (or small) as possible.
  • The highest peak = the global optimum \(x^*\). A lower peak is a local optimum: no step makes it better, yet it is not the best.

Maximising \(f\) is the same as minimising \(-f\): climbing a mountain = descending into the valley of the upside-down mountain.

Optimisation is the process of finding the best solution to a problem according to some criterion. It is used everywhere in science and engineering, and it always means finding the maximum or the minimum of a function. The analogy for today: you are a hiker in a mountain range and want to reach the highest point. Your position along the range is the decision variable x, the thing you can choose. The height at that position is the objective function f(x), the thing you want to make as large as possible. The highest peak is the global optimum, x*. The smaller peak on the left is a local optimum: standing there, every small step takes you down, so it looks like the best, but it is not. Many optimisation methods can get stuck on such local peaks; keep that in mind today. Maximising and minimising are the same problem: maximising f is the same as minimising −f. Most software (including SciPy) minimises, so to maximise we minimise the negative.

1The optimisation problem

objectiveconstraintsfeasible solutionoptimal solution

\(\displaystyle\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \ f(x)\)

subject to \(\ g_i(x) \le 0, \quad h_j(x) = 0\)

  • \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\), the objective (cost, fitness) function.
  • \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\), the decision variables.
  • \(g_i\), \(h_j\): inequality and equality constraints; together they define the decision space \(\mathcal{X}\).

A feasible solution satisfies every constraint. The optimal solution \(x^*\) is the feasible solution with the best objective:

\(f(x^*) \le f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathcal{X}\)

On the mountain: constraints are the fences of the park. The best point you may reach can be a peak inside the park, or a point on the fence.

This week: mostly unconstrained problems, plus simple bounds in SciPy.

The general form of an optimisation problem: minimise f(x) over the decision space, subject to constraints. f is the objective function, also called the cost or fitness function: it assigns a value to every possible decision. x is the vector of decision variables, n of them. The constraints define what is allowed: inequality constraints g(x) ≤ 0 (stress below the limit, budget not exceeded) and equality constraints h(x) = 0 (a required volume, a balance of forces). A feasible solution satisfies all constraints; there are usually many. The optimal solution is the feasible one with the best objective value. Mountain analogy: the constraints are the fences of a park. If the highest peak is inside the park, the answer is that peak; if it is outside, the best you can do is often a point on the fence. We will see that in the beam example next. This week we focus on unconstrained problems and simple bounds; constrained optimisation follows later.

1.1Engineering example: the lightest beam

minimise massdesign spaceactive constraint

A steel beam spans 6 m and carries 20 kN/m. Choose the width \(b\) and depth \(d\) of its rectangular section to minimise the mass, \(m \propto b\,d\), subject to:

  • bending stress below 165 MPa;
  • deflection below L/360;
  • \(d \le 6b\), so that it does not buckle sideways.

The lightest feasible design, 49 × 292 mm, weighs 112 kg/m. A square section that passes the same checks weighs 275 kg/m: 60 % more steel.

The optimum sits on the fence: where the deflection and stability constraints meet.

A first engineering example with two decision variables. A simply supported steel beam, span 6 m, uniform load 20 kN/m. We choose the width b and depth d of a solid rectangular section. Objective: minimise the mass, proportional to the area b × d. Constraints: the bending stress must stay below 165 MPa; the mid-span deflection below span/360; and the depth at most six times the width, otherwise a tall thin section would buckle sideways. The plot is the design space: every point is a possible beam. The purple curves are lines of equal mass. The green region contains the designs that satisfy all constraints. The lightest feasible design is where a mass curve just touches the feasible region: the star, 49 by 292 mm, 112 kg per metre. For comparison, a square section passing the same checks needs about 187 mm sides and weighs 275 kg/m. Optimisation saves 60 per cent of the steel, and its cost and embodied carbon. Note that the optimum is on the boundary (the fence), where the deflection limit and the stability limit meet. In constrained problems this is very common.

A second example, and a classic trade-off. A wind farm wants many turbines on a site (dense), but each turbine leaves behind a wake: a cone of slower, turbulent air. This is exactly the mountain: the decision variable is the spacing, the objective is the height, and we want the top.

Closed-form (analytical) solutions

When the objective is a known, differentiable formula, calculus can find the optimum exactly.

2.1At the top, the slope is zero

derivative f′(x)tangentstationary point

\(f(x) = \cos(2x)\, e^{-0.1x^2}\)

At every peak and every valley the tangent is horizontal: \(f'(x) = 0\). Solve this equation to find the candidates, the stationary points.

The key idea of analytical optimisation. Picture walking along the curve: going uphill the slope is positive, going downhill negative. Exactly at the top, the slope is zero: the tangent line is horizontal. The slope of the curve is the derivative f'(x). So the optimum must satisfy f'(x) = 0. Points where the derivative is zero are called stationary points. Widget: the damped cosine from the handout, f(x) = cos(2x) e^(−0.1x²) on [−5, 5]. Move the slider: the red line is the tangent at x, and the panel shows the slope. Find the places where it turns horizontal (marked with dots): the peaks and the valleys. Note: there are several stationary points here: maxima and minima, local and global. Setting the derivative to zero gives us the candidates; we then need to decide which one is the best. Next slide.

2.1Maximum or minimum? Worked example

second derivative f″(x)curvatureglobal optimum

Second-derivative test at a stationary point \(x_s\):

  • \(f''(x_s) < 0\): curved downwards, a maximum (a peak);
  • \(f''(x_s) > 0\): curved upwards, a minimum (a valley);
  • \(f''(x_s) = 0\): inconclusive.

Then compare the values of \(f\) at all candidates (and at the ends of the range) to find the global optimum.

Find \(\max f(x) = \cos(2x)\,e^{-0.1x^2}\) on \([-5, 5]\)

\(f'(x) = -e^{-0.1x^2}\big(2\sin 2x + 0.2x\cos 2x\big) = 0\)

\(e^{-0.1x^2} > 0\), so \(2\sin 2x + 0.2x\cos 2x = 0\). At \(x = 0\) both terms vanish: \(x_s = 0\).

\(f''(0) = -4.2 < 0 \ \Rightarrow\) a maximum, \(f(0) = 1\)

The other peaks (near \(x = \pm 3\)) are lower, about 0.39: \(x^* = 0\) is the global maximum.

The remaining roots of \(2\sin 2x + 0.2x\cos 2x = 0\) have no neat formula: a first hint that we will need numerical methods.

f'(x) = 0 finds peaks and valleys alike. To tell them apart, use the second derivative, the curvature. If f'' is negative, the curve bends downwards like a hill top: a maximum. If f'' is positive, it bends upwards like a valley: a minimum. If it is zero, the test says nothing. Finally, compare the objective at all candidates, and at the ends of the allowed range, to pick the global optimum. Worked example from the handout. Differentiate with the product and chain rules: f'(x) = −e^(−0.1x²)(2 sin 2x + 0.2x cos 2x). The exponential is never zero, so the bracket must be zero. x = 0 works: sin 0 = 0 and 0.2 × 0 = 0. Second derivative at 0: f''(0) = −4 − 0.2 = −4.2, negative, so a maximum, with f(0) = 1. The other peaks, near ±3, reach only about 0.39. So x* = 0 is the global maximum on [−5, 5]. Notice: the other stationary points need the equation 2 sin 2x + 0.2x cos 2x = 0, which has no closed-form solution. We can only find them numerically. Keep that in mind for section 3.

2.2Engineering example: the cheapest tank

objective with a constraintsubstitutionclosed form

A closed cylindrical tank must hold \(V = 10\ \text{m}^3\). Minimise the sheet metal, the surface area:

\(A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h, \qquad V = \pi r^2 h\)

Use the constraint to remove \(h = V/(\pi r^2)\):

\(A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \dfrac{2V}{r}\)

\(\dfrac{dA}{dr} = 4\pi r - \dfrac{2V}{r^2} = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ r^* = \Big(\dfrac{V}{2\pi}\Big)^{1/3} = 1.17\ \text{m}\)

\(h^* = 2r^* = 2.34\) m, \(A^* = 25.7\ \text{m}^2\). \(A''(r) = 4\pi + 4V/r^3 > 0\): a minimum.

An engineering problem that calculus solves exactly. A closed cylindrical tank must store 10 cubic metres. What radius and height use the least sheet metal? Objective: the surface area, two end discs plus the side: A = 2πr² + 2πrh. Constraint: the volume πr²h = V. Two variables and one equality constraint. Trick: use the constraint to eliminate one variable. h = V/(πr²). Substitute: A(r) = 2πr² + 2V/r, now a function of r only. Differentiate and set to zero: 4πr − 2V/r² = 0, so r³ = V/(2π) and r* = (V/2π)^(1/3) = 1.17 m. Then h* = V/(πr²) = 2.34 m, exactly twice the radius: the height equals the diameter. Surface area 25.7 m². Check: the second derivative 4π + 4V/r³ is always positive, so this is a minimum. The plot confirms it: the area is huge for thin tall tanks (small r) and for flat wide tanks (large r), with the minimum in between. Note the answer holds for any volume: the optimal closed cylinder has h = 2r. That is the power of a closed-form solution: a general formula, not just a number.

2.3Two variables: the gradient is zero at the top

partial derivativesgradient ∇f (Jacobian)tangent plane

A smooth mountain: the height \(h(x, y)\) depends on two variables.

The partial derivative \(\partial h / \partial x\) is the slope walking east; \(\partial h / \partial y\) the slope walking north. Together they form the gradient (for a scalar function, the Jacobian):

\(\nabla h = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial h}{\partial x} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial h}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}\)

The gradient points uphill, in the steepest direction. At the peak the ground is flat in every direction: the tangent plane is horizontal and

\(\nabla h(x^*, y^*) = \mathbf{0}\)

Now two decision variables, and a real mountain picture. The height h depends on the east coordinate x and the north coordinate y. In one dimension the slope was f'(x). In two dimensions there is a slope in each direction: the partial derivative with respect to x (walking east, keeping y fixed) and with respect to y (walking north, keeping x fixed). Stacked together, they form the gradient vector, written ∇h ("nabla h"). For a scalar function like h, the gradient is the same thing as its Jacobian (SciPy calls it jac, as we will see). The gradient has an important property: it points in the direction of steepest ascent, straight uphill, and its length is the steepness. At the summit of a smooth mountain the ground is flat in every direction: both partial derivatives are zero, so the gradient is the zero vector. The yellow grid is the tangent plane at the peak: horizontal. So the 1-D rule f'(x) = 0 becomes ∇h = 0: one equation per variable.

2.3Solving ∇h = 0 for the smooth mountain

system of equationsHessianstationary point

\(h(x, y) = -30x^2 - 20y^2 + 10xy + 210x + 80y + 660\)

Set both partial derivatives to zero:

\(\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial x} = -60x + 10y + 210 = 0\)

\(\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial y} = 10x - 40y + 80 = 0\)

Two linear equations, two unknowns: \(x^* = 4\) km, \(y^* = 3\) km, height \(h^* = 1200\) m.

Peak or valley? The Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, plays the role of \(f''\):

\(H = \begin{bmatrix} -60 & 10 \\ 10 & -40 \end{bmatrix}\)

Both eigenvalues negative (curved down in every direction): a maximum.

The smooth mountain in the picture is h(x, y) = −30x² − 20y² + 10xy + 210x + 80y + 660 (x and y in km, h in m). Differentiate with respect to x, treating y as a constant: −60x + 10y + 210. With respect to y, treating x as a constant: 10x − 40y + 80. Set both to zero. Two linear equations in two unknowns. From the second, x = 4y − 8; substitute into the first: −60(4y − 8) + 10y + 210 = 0, so −230y + 690 = 0, y = 3, and x = 4. The peak is at (4 km, 3 km), height 1200 m. In the contour map, the arrows are the gradient at many points: they all point uphill, towards the peak, and vanish there. Is it really a peak? In 2-D the second derivatives form the Hessian matrix: ∂²h/∂x² = −60, ∂²h/∂y² = −40, and the mixed derivative ∂²h/∂x∂y = 10. If the Hessian is negative definite (both eigenvalues negative, here about −64 and −36) the surface curves down in every direction: a maximum. Positive definite: a minimum. Mixed signs: a saddle, like a mountain pass. With n variables: n equations ∇f = 0 and an n × n Hessian. For a quadratic like this the equations are linear and easy. In general they are not.

Iterative methods

When the equations f′(x) = 0 or ∇f = 0 cannot be solved by hand: climb the mountain step by step.

3.1When there is no closed-form solution

iterative methodinitial guessconvergence

Analytical methods need a formula that we can differentiate and an equation \(f'(x) = 0\) that we can solve. Often we cannot:

  • No algebraic solution: \(f(x) = x\cos 3x\) gives \(\cos 3x - 3x\sin 3x = 0\).
  • No formula at all: the objective comes from a simulation (a Monte Carlo model, a finite element analysis, a crew schedule).
  • Too many variables: thousands of equations \(\nabla f = 0\).

Iterative methods start from an initial guess and improve it step by step, each step a little better, until the steps become tiny (convergence). They give a (near) optimal answer, usually a local one.

A hiker in fog cannot see the peak, but can feel the slope under their feet: step uphill, feel again, repeat.

Closed-form solutions are fast and exact, but they need a differentiable formula and a derivative equation we can actually solve. In engineering that is often not the case. First, the equation may have no algebraic solution. The handout's example f(x) = x cos(3x): its derivative cos 3x − 3x sin 3x = 0 cannot be rearranged for x. Second, there may be no formula at all: the objective comes out of a simulation, like last week's Monte Carlo wind farm, a finite element model, or the productivity of a construction site as a function of the crew size. Third, real problems can have thousands of variables. Iterative methods solve this. Back to the mountain, now covered in fog: you cannot see the peak, but you can feel the slope of the ground under your feet. So take a step uphill, feel the slope again, take another step, and repeat. Each step gets a little higher; when the ground is flat, you stop. That is all an iterative method is: an initial guess, a rule for improving it, and a stopping criterion. They do not guarantee the exact optimum (they stop close to it), and they usually find a local optimum: the peak nearest to where you started.

3.2Gradient descent

gradientlearning rate αsteepest descent

To minimise \(f\), step against the gradient, downhill:

\(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_k)\)

  1. start from an initial guess \(x_0\);
  2. calculate the gradient (1-D: the slope \(f'(x_k)\));
  3. step a distance proportional to it, in the opposite direction;
  4. repeat until the steps are tiny.

\(\alpha > 0\) is the learning rate (step size). To maximise, step with the gradient: \(x_{k+1} = x_k + \alpha\nabla f(x_k)\).

The step is \(\alpha\) times the slope: big steps where it is steep, small steps near the bottom, where \(\nabla f \to 0\).

The simplest iterative method, and the workhorse of machine learning: gradient descent. The gradient points uphill. To minimise, we step in the opposite direction: x_{k+1} = x_k − α ∇f(x_k). In one dimension the gradient is just the slope f'(x_k). The algorithm: start from an initial guess x0; compute the slope; move against it by α times the slope; repeat until the steps (or the slope) become negligibly small. α is the learning rate, or step size: how far to move per unit of slope. Because the step is proportional to the slope, the method automatically takes large steps where the ground is steep and smaller steps as it approaches the bottom, where the slope tends to zero. For maximisation (the hiker climbing), step with the gradient instead: gradient ascent. Or minimise −f. Note the method only needs the first derivative: cheap to compute, even with thousands of variables. That is why it is so popular.

\(f(x) = x\cos 3x, \quad f'(x) = \cos 3x - 3x\sin 3x\)

3.3Newton’s method: use the curvature too

second derivativequadratic approximationHessian

Near \(x_k\), approximate \(f\) by a parabola (second-order Taylor expansion):

\(f(x) \approx f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x - x_k) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x_k)(x - x_k)^2\)

Jump straight to the stationary point of the parabola:

\(x_{k+1} = x_k - \dfrac{f'(x_k)}{f''(x_k)}\)

With \(n\) variables, the Hessian \(H\) (matrix of second derivatives) replaces \(f''\):

\(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - H(\mathbf{x}_k)^{-1}\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)\)

Mountain analogy: the hiker feels not only the slope but also how the ground curves, guesses the shape of the hill, and walks straight to where its top should be.

  • No learning rate to choose.
  • Very fast near the optimum: the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step.
  • Exact in one step for a quadratic function.

Newton finds stationary points, where \(f' = 0\): it may land on a maximum when you want a minimum. If \(f''(x_k) < 0\), the parabola opens downwards.

Gradient descent only uses the slope. Newton's method also uses the curvature, the second derivative. Idea: around the current point, approximate the function by a parabola that has the same value, slope and curvature: the second-order Taylor expansion. The parabola's stationary point is easy to find: set its derivative f'(x_k) + f''(x_k)(x − x_k) to zero, which gives x_{k+1} = x_k − f'(x_k)/f''(x_k). Jump there, build a new parabola, repeat. With several variables, the Hessian matrix replaces the second derivative and its inverse multiplies the gradient. (The handout writes this as a division by ∇²f, which is the 1-D version.) Mountain analogy: the hiker feels the slope and how the ground bends, imagines the shape of the hill, and heads straight for its imagined top. Advantages: no learning rate; very fast near the solution (quadratic convergence: the error is roughly squared each step); for a quadratic function the parabola is exact, so one step lands on the optimum. The catch, from the handout: Newton's method finds stationary points, where the derivative is zero, so it may converge to a maximum instead of a minimum. That happens when the curvature is negative: the parabola opens downwards and its stationary point is its top. It also needs the second derivatives, which are expensive with many variables (a Hessian of n × n entries).

Both find local optima from a starting point. Practical solvers combine the two ideas: quasi-Newton methods (BFGS) estimate the curvature from gradients.

Summary of the two methods. Gradient descent: first-order, uses only the gradient. Needs a learning rate. Cheap per step, robust, slow near the solution. Always heads downhill, so it finds a minimum (the one in whose valley it starts). Newton: second-order, uses the curvature too. No tuning parameter. Very fast once close (4 steps against 11 in our example), but each step needs second derivatives: for n variables an n × n Hessian, expensive for large n. It finds stationary points, which can be maxima or saddle points, and can misbehave when f'' is close to zero (huge jumps) or negative. Both are local methods: the answer depends on the starting point. In practice, libraries mix the ideas. Quasi-Newton methods like BFGS build an approximate Hessian from successive gradients: nearly Newton's speed at gradient descent's cost. That is SciPy's default, as we will see.

Optimisation by hand in Python

Both methods in a few lines of Python, printing every iteration.

4.1Gradient descent, step by step

for loopstopping criterionbreak

Two functions (week 4), a for loop (week 2) and the update \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha f'(x_k)\). The loop stops (break) when the step is smaller than \(10^{-6}\), or after 50 iterations. Watch \(f'(x)\) shrink to zero.

import numpy as np

def f(x):
    return x * np.cos(3 * x)

def grad(x):                    # f'(x)
    return np.cos(3 * x) - 3 * x * np.sin(3 * x)

x = -3.9                        # initial guess
alpha = 0.02                    # learning rate
print(" k         x        f(x)       f'(x)")
for k in range(1, 51):
    step = alpha * grad(x)
    x = x - step                # move against the gradient
    print("%2d %10.5f %10.5f %10.5f" % (k, x, f(x), grad(x)))
    if abs(step) < 1e-6:        # converged: tiny step
        break
Gradient descent coded by hand, for f(x) = x cos(3x). Two functions: the objective and its derivative, which we worked out by hand with the product rule. The loop: compute the step α f'(x), update x, print the iteration, and stop when the step is smaller than one millionth. range(1, 51) caps the number of iterations at 50, in case it never converges. Run it: 11 iterations, ending at x = −4.21510, f = −4.20198, and the slope f'(x) printed in the last column shrinks to almost zero. Exercises for the class: alpha = 0.01 (the handout's value) takes 26 iterations; alpha = 0.04 oscillates around the minimum before settling (21 iterations); alpha = 0.07 bounces between two points and never converges; alpha = 0.12 jumps out of the valley at the first step. Change the initial guess to 0.5 and it ends in the local minimum at 1.14.

4.2Newton’s method, step by step

One more function, the second derivative, and the update \(x_{k+1} = x_k - f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)\). Compare: about 4 iterations instead of 11, and \(f'(x)\) drops like \(10^{-1}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-7}\). Then try x = 0.5: \(f''(x) < 0\) at the end, a maximum.

import numpy as np

def f(x):
    return x * np.cos(3 * x)
def grad(x):                    # f'(x)
    return np.cos(3 * x) - 3 * x * np.sin(3 * x)
def hessian(x):                 # f''(x)
    return -6 * np.sin(3 * x) - 9 * x * np.cos(3 * x)

x = -3.9                        # initial guess
print(" k         x        f(x)      f'(x)   f''(x)")
for k in range(1, 21):
    step = grad(x) / hessian(x)
    x = x - step                # stationary point of the parabola
    print("%2d %10.5f %10.5f %10.1e %8.2f"
          % (k, x, f(x), grad(x), hessian(x)))
    if abs(step) < 1e-8:
        break
Newton's method by hand. The only differences from the previous slide: a third function, the second derivative (differentiate f'(x) once more), and the update uses f'/f'' instead of α f'. Run it from −3.9: it converges in a handful of iterations to the same minimum, x = −4.21510. The slope column (in scientific notation) shows the quadratic convergence: each step squares the error. The last column, f''(x) = 38.3, is positive: a minimum. Then x = 0.5: it converges quickly to x = 0.28681, f = 0.187, with f'' negative: a local maximum. Newton did exactly what we asked (find where the slope is zero), just not what we wanted. The handout's first example, f(x) = x², is worth trying too: replace the three functions by x**2, 2*x and 2. Newton reaches x = 0 in one iteration, because a quadratic is its own parabola; gradient descent needs many.

5.1What is in scipy.optimize?

Other useful functions:

  • minimize_scalar: one variable, optionally in an interval;
  • root, brentq: solve equations, e.g. \(f'(x) = 0\);
  • curve_fit: fit a model to data (least squares);
  • linprog: linear programming;
  • differential_evolution: global search for many local optima.

If you do not give the gradient (jac), SciPy estimates it numerically.

scipy.optimize collects tested implementations of many optimisation methods. The main entry point is minimize(f, x0, method=...): give it the objective, a starting point and the name of a method. The methods differ in what information they use. Nelder–Mead uses only function values: robust, no derivatives needed, good for noisy or simulation-based objectives. CG, the conjugate gradient method, is a refined gradient descent that chooses its own step sizes. BFGS, the default, is quasi-Newton: it estimates the curvature from gradients. Newton-CG is Newton's method and needs the gradient (jac) and optionally the Hessian (hess). L-BFGS-B and TNC accept lower and upper bounds on the variables; SLSQP and trust-constr also handle general constraints. Other tools: minimize_scalar for one variable; root and brentq to solve equations; curve_fit for least-squares fitting; linprog for linear programming; differential_evolution for global optimisation when there are many local optima. If you do not supply jac, SciPy approximates the gradient by finite differences: it works, but costs extra function evaluations. All of these minimise: to maximise, minimise −f.

5.2minimize with gradient and Newton methods

result.xresult.funresult.nitresult.success

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def f(x):                        # x is an array: x[0] is our variable
    return x[0] * np.cos(3 * x[0])
def jac(x):                      # gradient: an array
    t = x[0]
    return np.array([np.cos(3 * t) - 3 * t * np.sin(3 * t)])
def hess(x):                     # Hessian: a matrix
    t = x[0]
    return np.array([[-6 * np.sin(3 * t) - 9 * t * np.cos(3 * t)]])

x0 = np.array([-3.9])
res_cg = minimize(f, x0, method="CG", jac=jac)
res_nt = minimize(f, x0, method="Newton-CG", jac=jac, hess=hess)

for name, r in [("CG", res_cg), ("Newton-CG", res_nt)]:
    print("%-9s x = %.5f  f = %.5f  nit = %d  success = %s"
          % (name, r.x[0], r.fun, r.nit, r.success))

The variables are always a NumPy array, so f takes x and uses x[0], even with one variable.

jac returns the gradient as an array; hess the Hessian as a matrix.

The result object:

  • x: the optimum; fun: \(f\) there;
  • nit: number of iterations;
  • success: True if the solver converged. Always check it.
The same problem with SciPy. minimize always works with arrays of variables, even for one variable, so the objective receives an array x and uses x[0]. The gradient (jac) must return an array with one entry per variable, and the Hessian a matrix. Two methods: CG, a gradient method, and Newton-CG, Newton's method (it needs jac; we also give hess). Run it: both reach x = −4.21510, f = −4.20198, the same as our hand-written loops, CG in about 5 iterations and Newton-CG in about 4. Much faster than our plain gradient descent, because CG chooses its step sizes automatically. The result object has several useful fields: x, fun, nit, and success, which tells you whether the solver converged. As the handout notes, always check success before trusting x; message explains what happened.

5.3Bounds and maximisation: the turbine spacing

boundsL-BFGS-Bmaximise = minimise −f

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def objective(s, Ct=0.8, k=0.05, cost=0.04):
    # power of two turbines (units of one turbine)
    # minus the cost of spacing s (in rotor diameters)
    deficit = (1 - np.sqrt(1 - Ct)) / (1 + 2*k*s)**2   # Jensen wake
    return 1 + (1 - deficit)**3 - cost * s

res = minimize(lambda s: -objective(s[0]), x0=[3],
               method="L-BFGS-B", bounds=[(2, 15)])
print("best spacing: %.2f rotor diameters" % res.x[0])
print("objective: %.3f, success: %s" % (-res.fun, res.success))

SciPy only minimises: to maximise the objective, minimise its negative, and change the sign of res.fun back.

bounds=[(2, 15)]: one (lower, upper) pair per variable; spacing between 2 and 15 diameters. Methods with bounds: L-BFGS-B, TNC, SLSQP.

The answer, about 7.8 D, is the top of the curve on slide 6, found here without a formula for the derivative.

Back to the wind turbine spacing from the start of the lecture, now solved with SciPy. The objective: the power of both turbines (the downstream one reduced by the Jensen wake deficit) minus a cost of 0.04 per rotor diameter of spacing. Setting its derivative to zero by hand would be messy; SciPy does not need it. Two new ideas. First, maximisation: minimize can only minimise, so we pass the negative of the objective (a lambda that returns −objective), and flip the sign of res.fun when we report the best value. Second, bounds: a list with one (lower, upper) pair per variable. L-BFGS-B, TNC and SLSQP accept bounds; CG and Newton-CG do not. Run it: about 7.82 rotor diameters, objective 1.251, success True. Exactly the top of the curve in the widget on slide 6. In the handout's example, bounds also change the answer: minimising x cos(3x) from x0 = −4 with bounds (−4, 2) stops on the bound at x = −4, because the true minimum at −4.215 is outside the allowed range: the fence again.

Summary

optimum∇f = 0gradient descentNewtonscipy.optimize

  • Optimisation: find the decision variables that give the best objective, within the constraints: the top of the mountain.
  • Maximising \(f\) = minimising \(-f\). Watch out for local optima.
  • Closed form: solve \(f'(x) = 0\) (1-D) or \(\nabla f = \mathbf{0}\) (n-D); check \(f''\) or the Hessian.
  • When the equations cannot be solved, or there is no formula: iterative methods.
  • Gradient descent: \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)\); simple, cheap, needs a good learning rate.
  • Newton: \(x_{k+1} = x_k - f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)\); fast, but needs \(f''\) and may find a maximum.
  • scipy.optimize.minimize: many methods; give jac/hess if you have them, use bounds when needed, check success.
Recap. Optimisation finds the best value of an objective by choosing decision variables, subject to constraints. Maximise f by minimising −f. If we have a differentiable formula and can solve the equations, calculus gives the exact optimum: f'(x) = 0 in one variable, ∇f = 0 in several, with f'' or the Hessian telling maximum from minimum. Otherwise, iterative methods improve a guess step by step: gradient descent follows the slope, Newton's method also uses the curvature. Both find local optima from a starting point. In practice, use scipy.optimize: choose a method, give derivatives if available, add bounds if needed, and check success. Next week: constraints and more realistic engineering optimisation problems.
