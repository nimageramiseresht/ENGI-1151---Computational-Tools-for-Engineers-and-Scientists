The theory of Monte Carlo simulation

Part A: what Monte Carlo simulation is, why we need it, random numbers, and how to read the results.

1What is Monte Carlo simulation?

Monte Carlo simulationrandom samplingtrial

A computational method that uses repeated random sampling to estimate a result.

  • Run the same calculation thousands of times, each time with inputs drawn at random.
  • Study all the answers together: their range, most likely value, spread.
  • Works for any model you can write as code.

Named after the Monte Carlo casino in Monaco: it relies on chance. Developed in the 1940s by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann to study neutron diffusion.

Warm-up: estimate π by throwing random points at a square. The share that lands inside the quarter circle is π/4.

Monte Carlo simulation is a technique that uses randomness to solve problems, even problems that are deterministic in principle. Instead of solving with an exact formula, we repeat a calculation many times with random inputs and approximate the answer from all the results. The name comes from the Monte Carlo casino because the method relies on chance. It was developed in the 1940s on the Manhattan Project by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann to study neutron diffusion. Warm-up widget: points are thrown uniformly at random into the unit square. The quarter circle has area π/4 and the square has area 1, so the share of points inside the circle approaches π/4. Four times that share estimates π. Click +1 a few times, then +100, +1 000, +10 000. With few points the estimate jumps around; with many it settles near 3.14. That is the whole idea of Monte Carlo: more trials, better estimate. Note the estimate improves slowly: 100 times more points gives only about 10 times smaller error (error shrinks like 1/√N).

1Why do we need it?

uncertaintybest guessrisk

Engineering inputs are rarely known exactly: loads, material properties, traffic, durations, costs.

One calculation with one best guess per input gives one answer, but not how far off it could be.

The questions we really need to answer:

  • What range of outcomes is possible?
  • What is the most likely outcome?
  • What is the chance of exceeding a limit?

Exact probability maths only works for simple models. Monte Carlo works for any model you can code.

Almost every input in an engineering calculation is uncertain: the load on a floor, the stiffness of steel, the number of vehicles on a road, how long a task takes, how much it costs. The usual approach is to plug in one best-guess value per input. That gives one answer, but tells us nothing about how wrong it could be. Example on the right (we will code this in Part B): a floor beam. With the average load and average stiffness, the deflection is 5.0 mm, comfortably below a 6 mm limit. But load and stiffness vary. Simulating 100 000 beams with random load and stiffness shows that about 11 per cent of them deflect more than 6 mm. The best guess hid a 1-in-9 chance of failing the limit. The questions we care about are about the whole distribution: range, most likely value, chance of exceeding a limit. For simple cases probability theory gives exact answers; for realistic models it quickly becomes impossible. Monte Carlo works for any model you can write as code.

2Types of uncertainty

deterministicstochasticresolutional (fuzzy)

Deterministic

The value is known exactly. The same inputs always give the same output.

e.g. the span of a beam set by the design, L = 6 m; g = 9.81 m/s²

Stochastic (random)

The value varies from one occurrence to the next. We describe it with a probability distribution.

e.g. daily traffic volume, wind speed, the strength of concrete cubes

Resolutional (fuzzy)

The value is vague, given in words: “about 100 km/h”. A fuzzy number gives each value a membership between 0 and 1.

e.g. expert judgement: “the delay will be about two weeks”. Just a hint: not covered further in this module.

We meet three kinds of uncertainty in engineering models. Deterministic: no uncertainty. The value is known exactly, and the same inputs always give the same output. A design dimension such as the span of a beam, or a physical constant such as g. Everything we have computed so far in this module has been deterministic. Stochastic, or random: the value changes from one occurrence to the next in a way we cannot predict exactly, but we can describe how likely each value is with a probability distribution. Traffic volume, wind speed, concrete strength measured on test cubes. This is the uncertainty Monte Carlo simulation handles, and today's topic. Resolutional, or fuzzy (just a hint): the value is not random but imprecise, because it is described in words or by limited resolution: "about 100 km/h". Fuzzy set theory represents it with a fuzzy number; the triangular fuzzy number shown gives 100 full membership (1) and values further away less membership, down to 0 at 90 and 110. Note the vertical axis is membership, not probability. Fuzzy methods are beyond this module; I mention them so you know the word.

3Two cars, step 1: crisp numbers

crisp numberrelative speed

Two cars travel in opposite directions. Their relative speed is the sum of their speeds.

With crisp (exact) numbers this is deterministic: one input, one answer. But does a driver ever hold exactly 90 km/h?

We will follow one simple example through the whole of Part A, and make it more realistic step by step. Two cars drive towards each other. Their relative speed, the speed at which the gap between them closes, is the sum of the two speeds. Step 1: crisp numbers, meaning single exact values. Car A at 90 km/h, car B at 110 km/h: the relative speed is 200 km/h. Deterministic, one answer, done. But no driver holds exactly 90 km/h, and we do not know in advance how fast a particular driver will go. The speeds are random. What happens to the answer then?

3Two cars, step 2: both speeds uniform

uniform distributioninterval arithmetic

Interval arithmetic adds the limits: the range is 130 to 264 km/h. But it says nothing about which values are likely.

Probability theory gives the full answer: a triangle. Values near 197 km/h are common, the extremes are rare. Still solvable by hand.

Step 2: we only know that each car drives somewhere between 65 and 132 km/h, any speed in between being equally likely. That is the uniform distribution U(65, 132): a flat rectangle. Interval arithmetic simply adds the limits: the slowest pair gives 130, the fastest pair 264 km/h. Useful, but it treats 130 and 197 as equally plausible, which they are not. Why a triangle? To get 130 both cars must be at their slowest: only one combination. To get 197 there are many combinations (65 + 132, 100 + 97, 132 + 65, …). So middle values are much more likely than extremes. The sum of two independent uniform variables is exactly triangular. This can be derived by hand with a convolution integral, so a closed-form answer still exists. Next step it does not.

3Two cars, step 3: realistic distributions

beta distributionnormal distributionclosed form

The exact answer needs a convolution integral:

fR(r) = ∫ fA(v) · fB(r − v) dv

For a beta plus a normal there is no closed-form result. With more inputs or a non-linear model it gets hopeless.

Too complex for pencil and paper → compute it instead: let the computer repeat the experiment thousands of times. That is Monte Carlo simulation.

Step 3: realistic speeds. Car A is a cautious driver: speed between 60 and 140 km/h but mostly around 70 to 90. We model that with a beta distribution, which lives between 0 and 1, scaled to 60 + 80 × Beta(2, 5). It is skewed: a long tail towards high speeds. Car B follows a normal distribution with mean 100 km/h and standard deviation 12 km/h: the familiar bell curve. What is the distribution of the sum? In theory it is the convolution integral shown. In practice, for a beta plus a normal there is no closed-form answer; you would need numerical integration. And this is only two inputs added together; a real model has many inputs combined non-linearly. So we stop trying to derive the answer analytically and compute it: simulate thousands of pairs of drivers and look at the results. That is Monte Carlo simulation. We will code exactly this example at the end of Part A.

4The Monte Carlo process

input distributionsmodeloutput distribution

Every Monte Carlo simulation has the same structure. 1. Inputs: each uncertain input is described by a probability distribution. 2. Random sample: draw one value of each input at random from its distribution. Here v_A = 78.4 and v_B = 106.1. 3. Model, or process: compute the output for this one set of inputs. Here it is just r = v_A + v_B = 184.5, but it can be any equation, a Python function, or a huge simulation model. The model itself is deterministic: the randomness comes only from the inputs. Repeat steps 2 and 3 N times, typically thousands or millions, storing every output. 4. Collection: the N outputs together form a new distribution, the distribution of the output. We plot it as a histogram and summarise it with statistics.

Steps 3 and 4 are where Python helps: NumPy draws thousands of random numbers in one line, and a function or array operation processes all of them.

The process as a recipe of five steps; use it to structure any Monte Carlo problem, including the coursework. 1. Define the problem: what output do you want, and which inputs are uncertain? 2. Assign a probability distribution to each uncertain input: from data, from standards, or from expert judgement. 3. Generate many random samples of the inputs. The next section shows how computers make random numbers. 4. Compute the output for every sample. A Python function (week 4) or array arithmetic (week 3). 5. Analyse the results: histogram, summary statistics, and the probability of exceeding a limit. Section 6.

Random numbers

Step 3 of every simulation needs random numbers. Where do they come from, and how do we get them in Python?

5.1True random and pseudorandom numbers

true randompseudorandomPRNGseed

True random numbers

From a physical process: dice, coin tosses, radioactive decay, atmospheric or thermal noise.

+ truly unpredictable (cryptography, lotteries)

− slow, need special hardware
− cannot be repeated to check a result

Pseudorandom numbers

From an algorithm, a pseudorandom number generator (PRNG), that starts from a number called the seed.

+ fast and lightweight
+ reproducible: same seed → same numbers
+ pass statistical tests of randomness

− deterministic: predictable if you know the algorithm and seed
− periodic: eventually the sequence repeats

A good PRNG has a long period, produces uniformly distributed numbers, is efficient and is reproducible. Simulations use pseudorandom numbers; from here on we just call them random numbers.

Random numbers are the raw material of every Monte Carlo simulation. There are two kinds. True random numbers come from physical processes that are unpredictable: rolling dice, radioactive decay, atmospheric or electronic noise. They are genuinely unpredictable, which matters for cryptography and lotteries. But they are slow to generate, need special hardware, and you cannot repeat the same sequence to check a result. Pseudorandom numbers come from a deterministic algorithm, a pseudorandom number generator or PRNG. It starts from a seed value and produces a sequence that looks random and passes statistical tests, but is completely determined by the seed. Advantages: fast, lightweight, and reproducible, which is essential for debugging and for checking someone else's simulation. Drawbacks: predictable if you know the algorithm and seed, and periodic: after enough numbers the sequence repeats. A good PRNG has a long period, uniform output, efficiency and reproducibility. Built-in generators: rand/srand in C, the random module in Python, rand/randi in MATLAB, and NumPy's np.random, which we use.

5.2Inside a PRNG: the linear congruential generator

LCGmod (remainder)period

Xn+1 = (a · Xn + c) mod m     un = Xn / m  in [0, 1)

mod m: the remainder after dividing by m, e.g. 16 mod 16 = 0, 31 mod 16 = 15. Python: %

first 20 numbers X0, X1, …

each number against the next (first 1 000)

The simplest PRNG is the linear congruential generator, LCG. Each number is computed from the previous one: multiply by a, add c, take the remainder after dividing by m. Dividing by m scales the numbers to between 0 and 1. Lecture example: a = 5, c = 1, m = 16, seed 3. Work out the first two by hand with the class: (5 × 3 + 1) mod 16 = 16 mod 16 = 0; (5 × 0 + 1) mod 16 = 1; then 6, 15, 12, ... After 16 numbers we are back at 3 and the sequence repeats: the period is 16. The scatter plot shows only 16 points: clearly not random enough for a simulation. Change the seed: a different sequence, but the same seed always gives the same sequence. That is reproducibility. Poor choice preset (a = 1, c = 1): the numbers just count up. Perfectly uniform, but obviously not random: uniformity alone is not enough. Large preset (a = 7^5 = 16807, c = 102435, m = 2^31 − 1, as in the lecture code): a very long period and the scatter plot fills the square evenly. Real generators (NumPy uses PCG64 by default; Python's random uses the Mersenne Twister) are more sophisticated, with periods like 2^128 or more.

5.3From U(0, 1) to any distribution

U(0, 1)scale and shiftinverse transform

Random numbers on [0, 1) are the building block for every other distribution.

U(a, b) = a + U(0, 1) · (b − a)

65 + 0.40 × (132 − 65) = 91.8 km/h

Scale first, then shift. (u + a) * (b - a) is a common mistake: it does not give values between a and b.

Any distribution: inverse transform x = F−1(u), where F is the cumulative distribution. Exponential, λ = 1.5: x = −ln(1 − u) / λ

Every random variable can be generated from uniform random numbers between 0 and 1, so U(0, 1) is the only thing a PRNG really needs to produce. Uniform between a and b: scale u by the width b − a, then shift by a. With u = 0.40 and the car speeds: 65 + 0.40 × 67 = 91.8 km/h. Watch the order: (u + a)(b − a) shifts first and then scales the shift too; for a = 0.6, b = 0.9 it gives values between 0.18 and 0.48 instead of 0.6 to 0.9. The original handout had this error; the notes on week5.html are corrected. For any other distribution, the inverse transform method: take the cumulative distribution F(x), the probability of a value up to x, which climbs from 0 to 1. Pick u on the vertical axis and read across and down to find x. Example from the lecture: exponential distribution with λ = 1.5, F(x) = 1 − e^(−λx), so x = −ln(1 − u)/λ. For u = 0.46: x = 0.411. Click "sample 1 000 values": 1 000 uniform u values are pushed through the curve, and their histogram follows the exponential pdf λe^(−λx). Steep parts of F collect many values; flat parts few. Another method in the lecture notes, rejection sampling, is used for distributions like the normal whose F has no simple inverse. You do not need to code these: NumPy does it for you (next slide).

Running and reading a simulation

Now we run the two-cars experiment many times and learn how to read the pile of results: histograms and statistics.

6.1The repeated experiment

trialnumber of trials Nconvergence

One trial: draw vA = 60 + 80·Beta(2, 5) and vB ~ N(100, 12), then add them.

Back to the two cars with the realistic distributions. One trial: draw a random speed for each car from its distribution and add them. Click +1 trial a few times. Each trial gives a different relative speed. One trial tells us almost nothing: it could be 165 or 210. Click +10, then +100: the histogram starts to take shape and the running mean wanders less. Click +10 000: the histogram becomes a smooth shape, the distribution of the relative speed: centred near 183 km/h, from about 120 to 250, slightly skewed to the right because of car A's long tail. This is convergence: the more trials, the more stable the results. The error of the mean shrinks like 1/√N, so 100 times more trials gives about 10 times more precision. That is why simulations use thousands to millions of trials, which only computers can do. Reset and repeat: a new random history, but with many trials the same final shape.

A histogram is how we see the distribution of simulation results.
The range of the data, from the smallest to the largest value, is split into equal bands called bins. We count how many results fall into each bin, and draw a bar whose height is the count.
Here: 30 relative speeds from the simulation, shown as dots on the number line. Move the slider. With 2 or 3 bins the shape is hidden; with 12 bins for only 30 values most bins hold 0, 1 or 2 values and the picture is noisy. In Python, plt.hist(r, bins=6) draws the histogram; np.histogram gives the counts and the bin edges as arrays. With density=True, each bar is divided by N × bin width, so the total area is 1 and the histogram can be compared directly with a probability density function, as we do in 6.6.

A histogram is a picture; statistics summarise it in a few numbers.
Mean: add all results and divide by N. It is the balance point of the histogram.
Median: sort the results and take the middle one; half the results are below, half above.
Standard deviation: the typical distance of the results from the mean; it measures spread. NumPy's np.std divides by N (population formula); np.std(x, ddof=1) divides by N − 1 (sample formula). With thousands of simulation results the difference is negligible.
For a symmetric distribution mean and median are equal. For a skewed one, like the travel-time histogram on the left with a long right tail, the mean is pulled towards the tail.
Run the code: 15 commute times. Mean 35.7, median 34.0, std 7.1. One accident day (61 minutes) pulls the mean up by almost 2 minutes and inflates the standard deviation, while the median does not care. Change 61 to 34 and run again.
  







  
6.4Mode and skewness

  
modeskewnesssymmetric

  

    

      
Mode: the most frequent value. Most useful for discrete results (counts); for continuous results it is the tallest bar of the histogram.

      
Skewness (advanced): which way the long tail points. g = mean of ((x − x̄)/σ)³

      
    

    
import numpy as np

# trucks arriving at a weighbridge, per 5 minutes
trucks = np.array([3, 2, 4, 3, 5, 3, 2, 1, 3, 4,
                   2, 3, 6, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2, 5, 3])
values, counts = np.unique(trucks, return_counts=True)
print("values:", values)
print("counts:", counts)
print("mode:  ", values[np.argmax(counts)])

times = np.array([32, 35, 31, 34, 38, 33, 36, 30,
                  34, 61, 35, 33, 37, 34, 32])
z = (times - np.mean(times)) / np.std(times)
print("skewness:", round(np.mean(z**3), 2))


  

  

Two more descriptors.
Mode: the value that occurs most often. It is natural for discrete results such as counts: the number of trucks arriving per 5 minutes is most often 3. np.unique with return_counts=True lists each value and how often it occurs; argmax finds the position of the largest count. For continuous results every value is different, so the mode is read from the tallest histogram bar instead.
Skewness (for those who want more): it measures asymmetry. Standardise each value (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation), cube it and average. Cubing keeps the sign, so a long right tail gives a positive skewness, a long left tail negative, and a symmetric distribution zero.
Rule of thumb from the pictures: right-skewed means mean > median; left-skewed means mean < median.
Run: the commute times have skewness 2.97, strongly right-skewed, all because of the one accident day. The two cars' relative speed from 6.1 has skewness of about 0.2: slightly right-skewed, from car A's tail.

Outlier? Check it. A real rare event: keep it, it is often the risk you care about. An impossible value (a negative number of cars): fix the input model.

    

  

  

The mean and standard deviation describe the middle; engineering decisions often depend on the edges.
Range: minimum to maximum. With many trials it grows, because rare extremes eventually appear, so on its own it is not a stable summary.
Percentiles: the 25th percentile (Q1) has a quarter of the results below it, the 50th is the median, the 75th is Q3. The 95th percentile is often used as a design value: only 5 per cent of outcomes are worse.
Probability of exceeding a limit: the fraction of trials above it. np.mean(x > limit) works because the comparison gives True/False values, counted as 1/0. We will use this in Part B.
Outliers: values far from the rest. The box plot rule: anything more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the box. Here the box is 32.5 to 35.5, IQR 3, upper fence 40, so the 61-minute accident day is an outlier. plt.boxplot(times) draws this plot.
What to do with outliers? Never delete them automatically. In a simulation, extreme results may be the rare failures you are looking for. But they can also reveal a modelling mistake: in the two-lane highway example in the notes, a normal distribution for the number of cars occasionally produces a negative number of cars, which creates absurd speeds. The fix there is a better input distribution, not deleting results.
  







  
6.6Comparing with the normal distribution

  
normal distributionstandard normalz-scorepdf

  

    
Normal pdf
f(x) = 1σ√(2π) e−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)

    
Standard normal N(0, 1): μ = 0, σ = 1.
Any normal value → z = (x − μ) / σ

    
68–95–99.7 rule: share of values within ±1σ, ±2σ, ±3σ of the mean

  

  
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

z = np.random.randn(10_000)        # standard normal samples
x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
pdf = np.exp(-x**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)

plt.hist(z, bins=40, density=True, color="skyblue",
         edgecolor="black", label="10 000 samples")
plt.plot(x, pdf, color="red", linewidth=2, label="N(0, 1) pdf")
plt.xlabel("z")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
print("within ±1σ:", np.mean(np.abs(z) < 1))


  

The normal distribution describes many natural phenomena (measurement errors, material properties, sums of many small effects), so we often compare simulation results against it.
Its probability density function has two parameters: the mean μ, which sets the centre, and the standard deviation σ, which sets the width.
The standard normal has μ = 0 and σ = 1. Any normal value converts to it with the z-score z = (x − μ)/σ: how many standard deviations from the mean.
68–95–99.7 rule: about 68 per cent of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 per cent within two, 99.7 per cent within three.
Run the code: 10 000 standard normal samples, histogram with density=True, and the pdf formula on top. They match closely, and about 0.68 of the samples are within ±1.
For a simulation result, compute its mean and std, draw the normal pdf with those values over the histogram, and see if it fits. If the histogram is skewed, the normal curve will not fit, and statements like "95 per cent within ±2σ" no longer hold: use percentiles instead.

Context: a simply supported steel floor beam in a laboratory, span 6 m. Sensitive equipment means a tight deflection limit, L/1000 = 6 mm (a typical floor limit is L/360, but vibration-sensitive equipment needs much less).
Mid-span deflection under a uniform load: δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI), the function students wrote in week 4.
With the average load (20 N/mm) and E = 200 000 N/mm², δ = 5.0 mm, below the limit. But the imposed load varies (std 3 N/mm) and the stiffness of steel varies slightly (std 10 000 N/mm²).
Steps: write the function; w = np.random.normal(20, 3, N); E = np.random.normal(200_000, 10_000, N); d = max_deflection(w, 6000, E, 3.375e8), which works element-wise on arrays because the function only uses arithmetic (week 4, functions with arrays); then the statistics from section 6; plt.hist and plt.axvline(6, color="red", linestyle="--"); np.mean(d > 6).

Discussion: about 11 per cent of beams would exceed the limit, more than the client's 5 per cent, so the beam is not acceptable even though the best-guess calculation said fine. Summary

  
Monte Carlo simulationrandom numbershistogramstatistics

  

    
    
      
  • Monte Carlo simulation: repeat a deterministic model many times with randomly sampled inputs; the outputs form a distribution.
    • 
      
  • Uncertainty can be deterministic, stochastic or resolutional (fuzzy); Monte Carlo handles stochastic inputs.
    • 
      
  • When the maths has no closed form (two cars, step 3), compute instead.
    • 
      
  • Five steps: define → assign distributions → sample → compute → analyse.
    • 
      
  • Computers use pseudorandom numbers: fast and reproducible with a seed. U(0, 1) builds every other distribution.
    • 
    

    
    
      
  • More trials → more stable results (error ∝ 1/√N).
    • 
      
  • Histogram: bins and counts; density=True for area 1.
    • 
      
  • Mean, median, std; mode for counts; skewness for asymmetry.
    • 
      
  • Percentiles and the chance of exceeding a limit drive engineering decisions; check outliers, do not delete them.
    • 
      
  • Compare with the normal distribution using the same mean and std.
    • 
    

  

  

Recap of the key ideas. A Monte Carlo simulation is a deterministic model run many times with random inputs; the collection of outputs is the answer.
Use it whenever inputs are random and the exact probability maths is too hard, which is most real problems.
The analysis tools (histogram, mean, median, standard deviation, percentiles, exceedance probability) apply to any data, not only simulations: you will use them in labs and projects.
